The struggle, as Jaspers says, is radical and ruthless. But — because of this very fact, not despite of it, it is a loving conflict; for what I want is not victory, not destruction of my opponent, but both for him and for myself that truth that neither he nor I can capture in rational apodictic language.

We are not concerned with winning the argument. Our concern is never to forsake the use of reason in order to reach the richer possibilities of what lies beyond any reason. Jaspers has shown over and over again how Transcendence escapes from Reason — by caging it in the superficial enclosures of circles, contradictions, and tautologies — thus manifesting itself to us.

The liberty must not be stifled or diminished by any kind of institution — neither a political nor a religious institution. On the contrary: the only justification for any institution lies in the possibilities it secures for this liberty. This is the standard that Jaspers consistently sets for his political views — the profound, the absolute root of his opposition to any kind of attempted totalitarianism. And if there is nothing of the anarchist in his make-up — if he lays great stress on the necessity for Authority — that is because he defends metaphysical liberty, not in the realm of words or literature, but in that of specific decisions in a given historical situation and here his studies and experience have taught him that in human societies Freedom without Authority entails the self-destruction of Freedom.

In his anxiety for the preservation of Liberty Jaspers, shortly before Hitler seized power, published a serious warning: he had realized that an empty, insubstantial, abstract Freedom would not be able to withstand any attack by violence, any temptation to cowardly abdication. After the years of silence and ganger under Hitler's rule, immediately after Germany's defeat, Jaspers gave his lecture on the Guilt Question. In the midst of the ruins of the German Reich and of the cities, he strode straight forward to the most urgent of all tasks, i. e. to obtain a clear view, unobscured by partiality or by masochism, of what had been done, in order to pave the way for the resurgence of true Liberty. There could hardly be a more impressive view of the nature of the relations between the existential urge towards Transcendence and the relentless search for clarity.
