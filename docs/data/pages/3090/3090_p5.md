It means more than this: the effect of his work on future generations will certainly be influenced by the fact that he has been able to defy the verdict of the doctors who, when he was twenty years old, told him he had no future; that he mastered so completely the art of living with his illness and was yet able to create one of the greatest and most important works of our time; that he lived through all the crises which shattered his own country, Europe, the world, and the whole epoch — by preserving in each new situation his unsullied clarity of vision and steadfastness of decision.

This would not be so important in the case of other philosophers. And I am not now referring to those whose lives one would prefer to forget because these are so little compatible with the style of their work and the aspirations expressed therein — whose genius appears to be the result so to speak of a sudden inspiration, a mercifully accorded gift, not a product of their own freedom — as if indeed some other person had been guiding the pen as they wrote. But even in the case of those thinkers whose signature is so much a part of their work that the work’s main function is to maintain their presence amongst us — even in these cases we may be confronted with the short-lived glow of a single flame, of an agonized outcry, a moment of ecstasy. This has the effect of fixing for all eternity (as in a mask) the most poignant moment of a tragic crisis or of a revelation — and then it is quite unimportant whether or not the author lives on.

The case of Karl Jaspers is very different. His philosophy, like that of a Socrates, a Spinoza, or a Kant, is derived, not from a single momentary experience, but from the efforts of a lifetime, and from the character of what emerges therefrom. Time, with its wearying repetitions which put everything to the test and threaten erosion, is a decisive factor in the moulding of quality; it is not the surprising, the unexpected that prevents style from banality, but faithfulness. Style, that monotony which speaks of being.

There is no other thinker in whom philosophy takes on such a superb — one might almost say such a sacred — meaning, while at the same time being anything but self-sufficient. For Jaspers, philosophy is of ultimate seriousness. Our whole being is bound by it. Indeed, it is itself our bond, and yet also the ever more clearly emerging consciousness that we derive from it and its absolute character.
