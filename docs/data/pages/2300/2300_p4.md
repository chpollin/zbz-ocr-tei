It means that, located in time, Existence can only be as related to this “beyond”. It always stands in the presence of Transcendence, knowing that it has not created itself, but that it is to itself a gift of Transcendence. Indeed, Existence has not created itself, nor can it be derived from anything else but *Transcendence*. For Jaspers, these two notions are correlated and neither of them has any meaning without the other.

As I said, Transcendence is sometimes called God. But it cannot be known, as God is claimed to be known in specific institutionalized churches, thanks to a Revelation or a Book. Transcendence is not a compensation for the failure of the quest of total and unified knowledge. It is a presence, but in no way a possession. On the contrary: if it is God, it is the hidden God — *deus absconditus*.

Let us try now to understand — or imitate — the central gesture of Jaspers. It is, first, an attempt to reach the totality and unity of being as world or as self. This is an extremely demanding and critical attempt, so demanding and so critical that it leads to the experience of its impossibility: every science is engaged in a search without end; every science is relative to its specific viewpoint and methods, so that altogether the sciences do not constitute a science of the whole. They are objective sciences. Even if they try to achieve a knowledge of the subject, it will be of the subject without its subjectivity — and there is no knowledge which could encompass the subject and the object; that is to say, which could reach being.

Instead of achieving a unified and total *knowledge* of being, the thinker experiences the failure of this knowledge — together with a call for an *absolute presence* — Existence in front of Transcendence.

The central gesture of Jaspers is a search for knowledge up to the limits which cannot be transgressed, the experience of failure at these limits and, because of this failure, the arising of a call for absolute presence, *hic et nunc*, of Existence in front of Transcendence.

In all the works of Jaspers, it seems to me, we find, in very different ways, this same gesture. There is a move towards a whole, which, in the human condition, cannot be grasped, and a transcending move which, without ever leaving the human reality, requires an absolute response. It is as if the essence of philosophising were to engage oneself into an “in-between”, *beyond* any *possible knowledge* of empirical reality, and *before* any *possible freedom*, giving itself up in a mystical unity.

Such a scheme is not without similarity, for instance, with the Platonic method of dialogue, with its constant move between the level of empirical things and the one of Ideas.
